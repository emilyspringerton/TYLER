## THE DUCK'S ACCOUNT

The city doesn't care what happened to you last night. Cities never do. Detroit especially — it's
seen worse than a sticky patch and a dead woman's half a sentence, and it'll see worse again
before the coffee gets cold.

I wasn't in that garage. I want that on the record before anybody accuses me of padding my own
résumé. But I know how these things go, because I've made a career — a modest one, forty-first
percentile, but a career — out of showing up right after the damage is done and telling people
what it meant.

Here's what it meant.

There's a tape. There's always a tape. The sound man — decent kid, doesn't know yet that decent
kids are the ones these stories chew up first — had four seconds of a voice that wasn't supposed
to exist recorded onto a device that was, at the time, pointed at a concrete floor like it already
knew better than to look up.

Four seconds. That's nothing. That's the time it takes a duck to decide whether a stranger's
sandwich is worth the risk. But four seconds of the *right* voice is a lifetime, if you know how
to listen to it, and I know how to listen to things, because listening is ninety percent of
telekinesis and the other ten percent is a story for another night.

They played it back in a room that used to be an office and was now mostly a folding table and a
laptop running software older than the crime it was trying to solve.

**HANA:** Clean it up. Whatever's under the hiss, I want it.

**CAM:** It's four seconds. There's not a lot of "under" to get to.

**HANA:** Four seconds of a woman who already knew how this ends. Humor me.

Now — I wasn't in that room either, but I know Hana's voice when it goes flat like that, because
I've heard it go flat once before, on a bench, outside a factory, about a hoodie. That's not the
voice of a woman asking a favor. That's the voice of a woman filing a complaint against a version
of events she hasn't lived through yet.

The kid ran it. Noise floor down, the hiss combed out frequency by frequency, the way you'd pick a
sesame seed off a very small, very specific piece of toast. And underneath it — not louder,
exactly, just *clearer*, the way a lie gets clearer the fourth time somebody tells it — four
words where there used to be three and a half.

**DON'T LET HIM START—**

Cut. Still cut. Whatever came after "start" belonged to a woman standing in a room that had
already stopped being their room by the time the word finished leaving her mouth.

**TYLER (on the tape, weeks-old, played back over this):** Somebody started at yellow.

**HANA:** Yeah. I heard you the first time.

**CAM:** So she wasn't warning him not to touch the patch. She was warning him not to *start*
something.

**HANA:** Or somebody already had, and she was thirty seconds and one whole timeline too late to
stop it.

Here's my professional opinion, for whatever a duck's professional opinion is worth on a market
where I'm still fighting my way up from fifth-worst: nobody in that room wanted to say the next
sentence out loud, so nobody did. They just sat with "START—" hanging in the air like a word that
knew it wasn't finished and had made its peace with that.

The PA — good kid, better instincts than the paycheck deserves — asked the only honest question
left on the table.

**PA:** Start *what*?

Nobody answered. Not because nobody had a guess. Because everybody had the *same* guess, and
saying it out loud was the one thing that would've made it real before anyone was ready to file
the paperwork on real.

I'll tell you what I think, since nobody in that garage was willing to. I think the ignition
order doesn't care whose thumb starts it. Black just needs a thumb and a corner and somebody who
hasn't yet learned to be afraid of the right things in the right order. Tyler had all three. So
did whoever the older Hana was shouting at in a room Tyler was only halfway standing in.

Two people. One sequence. Only one of them gets to finish it clean.

That's the part nobody in that folding-table office wanted to say. I'm a duck in a fedora with
nothing on the line, so I'll say it: somebody's running the same five colors Tyler is, on a clock
none of them can see, and "wrong order" doesn't mean broken. It means *behind*.

## FRAME — AFTER THE TAPE

**TYLER:** That's not what happened.

**THE DUCK:** That's what happened *plus context*. The context is the good part. Anyone can report
four seconds of audio. I'm giving you a *read*.

**TYLER:** You gave everyone a guess and delivered it like a verdict.

**THE DUCK:** In my professional experience, that's the only way anyone in this ecosystem has ever
delivered anything. I learned from watching you people.

*A beat. The Frog, somewhere off-frame, unable to resist.*

**THE FROG:** He's not wrong that it's dramatic. He's not right that it's true.

**THE DUCK:** The fun version doesn't need a decimal point. Frog said that first, I'm reassigning
credit to myself, it's a coping mechanism, not a citation error.

**TYLER:** *(to camera, dry, done)* For the record: the audio's real. The word is real. Everything
after "somebody's running the same five colors" is a duck with a fedora filling in the parts
nobody's confirmed yet.

**THE DUCK:** And yet. You didn't correct the part about being behind.

Tyler doesn't answer that. Which, on this show, has never once meant nothing.

*Cut to black.*
